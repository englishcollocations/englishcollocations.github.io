Lesson 1 – Family

Let’s begin our study of collocations by learning some common expressions about the family. Your parents and siblings (brothers and sisters) are your <strong>immediate family</strong> – and your <strong>extended family</strong> includes all your relatives – uncles, cousins, great-aunts, etc.

You can use a <strong>family tree</strong> to diagram the relationships among your <strong>family members</strong>. A person who is related to you by a long series of connections can be called a <strong>distant relative</strong>.

If you’re lucky, you have a <strong>loving family</strong> or a <strong>close-knit family</strong> – these expressions refer to a family that has good relationships, where everyone loves each other and helps each other. If you were raised in a loving family, then you probably had a <strong>carefree childhood</strong> – that means you had nothing to worry about when you were young.

On the other hand, a family in which the relationships are bad or unhealthy can be called a <strong>dysfunctional family</strong>. If the children experience abuse, poverty, or problems with the law, we can say they had a <strong>troubled childhood</strong>.

Perhaps the parents went through a <strong>bitter divorce</strong> – that means a separation in which there were bad/angry feelings between the husband and wife. It’s also possible to have a <strong>messy divorce</strong>, with a <strong>prolonged legal battle</strong> involving lots of conflicts about the separation of the former couple’s assets (money and possessions). The decisions about the separation of assets are made in the <strong>divorce settlement</strong>. A family in which there are divorces or separations is sometimes called a <strong>broken home</strong>.

Sometimes the mother and father <strong>fight over custody</strong> of the children – that refers to who has the primary responsibility of caring for the kids. A judge can <strong>grant joint custody</strong> – that means the ex-husband and ex-wife share the responsibility – or <strong>sole custody</strong> to only one parent. For example, a judge might <strong>award sole custody</strong> to the mother, and the father has to pay <strong>child support</strong> – regular payments to help with expenses for the kids.

If it was a <strong>mutual divorce</strong>/<strong>separation</strong> – that means the ex-husband and ex-wife agreed to separate without fighting – then they will probably <strong>stay on good terms</strong> with each other (meaning to have a polite relationship without conflicts).

If a woman <strong>gets pregnant</strong> without being married or in a relationship, then she will become a <strong>single mother</strong>. Some women in this situation choose to have an abortion, and others prefer to <strong>give the baby up for adoption</strong>. The time when the baby is planned to arrive is called the <strong>due date</strong> – you can also say the <strong>baby is due</strong> in mid-October, for example.

After the woman <strong>has the baby</strong> (or <strong>gives birth to the baby</strong>), the baby is given to the <strong>adoptive parents</strong>, who will <strong>raise the child</strong> or <strong>bring up the child</strong> as if it was their own. Sometimes, when the adopted child is older, he or she tries to find their <strong>birth mother</strong> (<strong>biological mother</strong>).
