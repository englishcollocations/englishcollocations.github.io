Lesson 36 – Changes

To talk about making small changes, you can say <strong>make adjustments</strong>, <strong>make alterations</strong>, or <strong>make modifications</strong>. More informally, you can say <strong>make tweaks</strong> – "We made a few <strong>tweaks</strong> to the website."

To talk about bigger changes, you can use the verbs "<strong>transform</strong>" and "<strong>revolutionize</strong>":

The internet has <strong>revolutionized</strong> the way people communicate.
The city <strong>transformed</strong> the vacant lot <strong>into</strong> a playground.
(you usually "<strong>transform</strong>" one thing "<strong>into</strong>" something else)

When you put on different clothes, you <strong>change your clothes</strong>. When you remove a baby's dirty diaper and put on a clean one, you <strong>change the baby's diaper</strong>. When you reverse a decision or opinion, then you <strong>change your mind</strong>. And when you introduce a different topic of conversation, then you <strong>change the subject</strong>. When you change your religion, then we often use the verb <strong>convert</strong> – I <strong>converted to Christianity</strong>; my brother wants to <strong>convert to Islam</strong>.

Other verbs used for changes are "<strong>turn</strong>," "<strong>become</strong>," and "<strong>get</strong>." Let's look at their different collocations.

Use <strong>turn</strong> with colors:
• Bananas <strong>turn black</strong> if you put them in the refrigerator.

You can also use <strong>turn into</strong> as a less formal expression for "transform into":
• Our living room couch <strong>turns into</strong> a bed.

Use <strong>become</strong> with emotions and states of being, including comparative adjectives:
• My sister <strong>became angry</strong> when she found out I'd broken her computer.
• It's <strong>becoming apparent/clear</strong> that the treatment isn't working.
• The band's music <strong>became popular</strong> among teenagers.
• It's <strong>becoming more difficult</strong> to separate one's personal and professional life.

You can also use <strong>become</strong> with professions: My brother <strong>became an actor</strong>.

The word <strong>get</strong> can be used as a less formal alternative to "become" with emotions and comparative adjectives (but NOT with professions or states of being).

• I'm <strong>getting excited</strong> about my upcoming trip to Spain.
• It's <strong>getting more expensive</strong> to rent a place in this neighborhood.
• Will it ever <strong>get easier</strong> for me to understand movies in English?
