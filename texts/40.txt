Lesson 40 – Cause & Effect

The word "cause" is primarily used with negative results: a storm can <strong>cause damage</strong>, a mechanical defect can <strong>cause a problem</strong>, unclear instructions can <strong>cause confusion</strong>, a political scandal can <strong>cause an uproar</strong> (a strong negative reaction/manifestation from the public), a high-risk sport can <strong>cause injury</strong>, and an infection in a hospital can <strong>cause death</strong>.

You can describe something as <strong>cause for concern</strong> or <strong>cause for alarm</strong> – meaning a reason for concern or alarm. For example, "The growing number of teenagers dropping out of school is <strong>cause for concern</strong>." The word "alarm" is a little stronger and more urgent: "The rapid spread of the virus is <strong>cause for alarm</strong>." One of the only positive collocations with "cause" is <strong>cause for celebration</strong>: "The opening of the new school was <strong>cause for celebration</strong> in the community."

When analyzing a problem, you can identify the <strong>main/primary cause</strong> of the issue – but keep in mind that there may be <strong>hidden/underlying causes</strong> as well. The deepest or most fundamental cause is sometimes called the <strong>root cause</strong>.

Interestingly, we do not use the verb "cause" together with "effect." Instead, we say that something <strong>has an effect</strong> – for example, "Television <strong>has a strong effect</strong> on public opinion." Another way to say "a strong effect" is the expression a <strong>significant impact</strong> or a <strong>profound impact</strong>.

There are many different ways to describe effects:

<strong>short-term vs. long-term effects</strong> – the time of the effect
<strong>visible effects vs. subtle effects</strong> – whether or not the effects are obvious
<strong>powerful effect vs. minimal/negligible effect</strong> – how strong the effect was
<strong>beneficial effect vs. adverse effect</strong> – to describe positive and negative effects
<strong>main effect vs. side effect</strong> – to describe the primary effect or an additional effect

Something can also <strong>have/produce results</strong> – the <strong>preliminary results</strong> are the early ones, and after more time passes, you'll get the <strong>final results</strong>. Results that obviously exist and can be proved are called <strong>concrete/tangible results</strong>, and a result that is impossible to avoid is an <strong>inevitable result</strong>. Results can also be <strong>direct</strong> or <strong>indirect</strong>, and the result that you want is called the <strong>desired result</strong>.

One word that is similar to "result" is "<strong>outcome</strong>" – it describes a resulting situation. You can try to guess the <strong>likely/probable outcome</strong> – the <strong>logical outcome</strong> is the result you would expect, and an <strong>unexpected outcome</strong> is one you didn't predict.
