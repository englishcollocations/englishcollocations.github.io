Lesson 38 – Starting / Finishing

Let's get started with a common question: is there a difference between the verbs "start" and "begin"?

There's no difference in meaning – "I <strong>started studying</strong> English 3 years ago" is the same as "I <strong>began studying</strong> English 3 years ago" – but "begin" is a little more formal than "start."

The phrase "<strong>let's get started</strong>" is an informal way to announce the beginning of an activity. You can also say that something "<strong>got off to a good start</strong>" if it began well. Other collocations for a good start include an <strong>encouraging start</strong> or <strong>promising start</strong> – these expressions show that there is hope for good results in the future. For example, "The soccer team got off to a <strong>promising start</strong>, winning the first four games of the season."

If it is not so certain that there will be good results in the future, then you can say a <strong>shaky start</strong> or <strong>unsteady start</strong> – "The business had a <strong>shaky start</strong> in the second quarter of 2013, with sales numbers fluctuating daily."

If the beginning of something did not meet expectations, you can call it a <strong>disappointing start</strong> – and if it was REALLY terrible, you can describe it as a <strong>disastrous start</strong>. "Kevin failed every single one of his first exams – his semester was off to a <strong>disastrous start</strong>."

Sometimes things go so badly that you need to get out of the situation and have a completely <strong>fresh start</strong> or <strong>new beginning</strong>. When a successful person or company started without being famous or rich, then we say they had a <strong>humble/modest beginning</strong>.

Now let's talk about "finish" and "end" – there is a difference between these two verbs. "<strong>Finish</strong>" usually implies completing something, whereas "<strong>end</strong>" is used for a more passive situation/state:

• I <strong>finished</strong> my homework.
• The semester <strong>ends</strong> in December.
• We need to <strong>finish</strong> the project.
• We <strong>ended</strong> our partnership with that company.

When you make the final changes to something before it is completed, we say you <strong>put the finishing touches</strong> on it.

Two expressions used to talk about ending something are <strong>come to an end</strong> or <strong>draw to a close</strong>. You can also say "<strong>the beginning of the end</strong>" to describe something that will probably end soon – "The explosion at the factory was <strong>the beginning of the end</strong> for our company." In other words, the explosion began a process of the company failing and eventually shutting down.

When something ends quickly, then it <strong>comes to an abrupt/sudden end</strong>. On the other hand, when an activity appears that it will continue forever, then we say there's <strong>no end in sight</strong>.
