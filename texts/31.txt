Lesson 31 – Texture

The word "texture" refers to the way something feels, and there are many different collocations used to describe it. Let's look at them in pairs of opposites.

<strong>Dry hair</strong> / <strong>Oily or greasy hair</strong> – You need to wash <strong>oily hair</strong> more often than you need to wash <strong>dry hair</strong>.

<strong>Dry skin</strong> / <strong>Oily skin</strong> – <strong>Oily skin</strong> is prone to acne, whereas <strong>dry skin</strong> often needs moisturizer.

A <strong>Smooth</strong> / <strong>Rough surface</strong> – Surfaces such as the ground, a road, and your skin can be <strong>smooth</strong> (with a consistent surface) or <strong>rough</strong> (with a varied surface).

<strong>Calm</strong> / <strong>Choppy or rough water</strong> – The water of the ocean is <strong>calm</strong> when the weather is mild, and it is <strong>choppy/rough</strong> when there are high winds.

<strong>Hard</strong> / <strong>Soft object</strong> – A mattress or pillow, for example, can be <strong>hard</strong> or <strong>soft</strong>.

<strong>Tender</strong> / <strong>Tough meat</strong> – Meat that is "<strong>tender</strong>" is easy to cut and chew, and meat that is "<strong>tough</strong>" is difficult to cut and chew.

A <strong>Sharp</strong> / <strong>Dull blade or point</strong> – A knife or a pencil can be "<strong>sharp</strong>" – meaning it can cut – or "<strong>dull</strong>," meaning it has lost its ability to cut.

The verbs "<strong>soften</strong>" and "<strong>harden</strong>" describe the process of becoming soft and becoming hard. For example, potatoes <strong>soften</strong> when you cook them in boiling water. And snow <strong>hardens</strong> if the temperature decreases and the snow begins turning into ice.

We can also use "<strong>soften</strong>" and "<strong>harden</strong>" in a more metaphorical way – we can say a person's <strong>voice softens</strong> when they begin speaking in a more friendly tone, and their <strong>voice hardens</strong> when they begin speaking in a less friendly tone. A person's attitude or views can also "<strong>soften</strong>" (become more friendly/tolerant) or "<strong>harden</strong>" (become less friendly/less tolerant).

Texture words are often used when describing food:

• <strong>creamy</strong> - describes a smooth and rich texture, like cream. Sauces, soups, puddings, and chocolate can be <strong>creamy</strong>.

• <strong>crunchy/crispy</strong> – describe something that makes a noise when you chew it. Crackers, chips, bacon, granola, and other "hard" foods can be <strong>crunchy</strong> or <strong>crispy</strong>.

• <strong>chewy</strong> – describes food that requires some effort to chew. Caramels and other types of candy can be <strong>chewy</strong>. This word can have a negative connotation if the food is not supposed to be <strong>chewy</strong> – for example, when you overcook meat, it becomes <strong>tough and chewy</strong>.

• <strong>greasy</strong> – describes food with a lot of oil. Hamburgers, French fries, and chicken wings can be <strong>greasy</strong>.
