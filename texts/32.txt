Lesson 32 – Smell

Today we'll continue learning collocations related to the five senses, with expressions for describing different smells. Let's begin by looking at some commonly confused words.

<strong>Aroma</strong>, <strong>scent</strong>, and <strong>fragrance</strong> all describe a good smell. You can talk about the <strong>wonderful aroma</strong> of a cake that has just been taken out of the oven, the <strong>fresh scent</strong> of flowers, and the <strong>sweet fragrance</strong> of a woman's perfume.

The word <strong>smell</strong> is neutral; you can have the <strong>lovely smell</strong> of fresh-baked bread, or the <strong>horrible smell</strong> of rotting food. The word "smell" can be a noun or a verb – so you can also say "<strong>It smells delicious</strong>" or "<strong>It smells nasty</strong>."

The words <strong>stench</strong>, <strong>stink</strong>, and <strong>odor</strong> all describe bad smells – there's the <strong>foul odor</strong> of a dead animal, and the <strong>revolting stench</strong> of sewage. The unpleasant smell of a person who doesn't take showers or use deodorant is called <strong>body odor</strong>.

To describe the strength of smells, you can say they are <strong>light/faint smells</strong> or <strong>strong smells</strong>. When a smell is extremely strong, you can say it is an <strong>overpowering smell</strong>. A smell that you recognize can be called a <strong>familiar smell</strong>, and a smell that is unique and easy to identify can be called a <strong>distinctive smell</strong> or an <strong>unmistakable smell</strong>.

Smells can "<strong>waft</strong>" – that means float through the air. For example, the <strong>mouth-watering scent</strong> of chocolate chip cookies <strong>wafted</strong> across the room. Another verb used with smell is "<strong>fill</strong>" – the <strong>acrid smell</strong> of smoke <strong>filled</strong> the hallway. Finally, if a smell stays in a place for a long time, we can use the verb "<strong>linger</strong>" – the <strong>spicy aroma</strong> of her cooking <strong>lingered</strong> in the kitchen for hours.

Here are some additional collocations for describing smells:

<strong>earthy/fishy/fruity/metallic smell</strong> – smells like soil/dirt, fish, fruit, or metal
<strong>rancid/putrid smell</strong> – a disgusting smell of things that are rotten
<strong>damp/dank smell</strong> – a smell of wet things and mold
<strong>a pervasive smell</strong> – a smell that tends to fill the space and remain there
<strong>a pungent smell</strong> – a smell that is "sharp" and strong; it almost hurts your nose when you smell it
