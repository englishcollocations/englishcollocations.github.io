Lesson 19 – Weather

Do you want to know if it'll be sunny or rainy next weekend? Then you should check the <strong>weather forecast</strong> – that's the prediction of the weather in the near future.

If the forecast says the <strong>sun will be shining brightly</strong>, with <strong>clear skies</strong> (no clouds in the sky) and high temperatures, then it's a good day to go to the beach and <strong>bask in the sunshine</strong> (or <strong>soak up the sunshine</strong>) – these expressions mean to enjoy the sun's heat and light.

However, if the forecast says it'll be <strong>oppressively hot</strong>, <strong>scorching hot</strong>, or <strong>swelteringly hot</strong>, then you might want to stay inside and turn up the air conditioning! Another common phrase is <strong>hot and humid</strong> – that means hot with a lot of water in the air (so you will sweat a lot).

The weather forecast might say it will be <strong>partly cloudy</strong> (the sky partially covered by clouds). Another way to say this is that there are <strong>scattered clouds</strong> (just a few clouds). If there are <strong>heavy clouds</strong>, then you won't be able to see the sun at all – and if there are <strong>storm clouds</strong>, then it will probably rain very soon.

One way to express this is to say "<strong>It looks like rain</strong>."

We can have <strong>light rain</strong> (little rain) or <strong>heavy rain</strong> (lots of rain, intense rain) – and if the rain gets REALLY intense, we can describe it as <strong>torrential rain</strong>. If the rain continues without stopping, then we call it <strong>continuous rain</strong> or <strong>constant rain</strong> – but if it's the type of rain that starts and stops various times throughout the day, it's called <strong>intermittent rain</strong> or <strong>scattered showers</strong>. When it stops raining, you can say the <strong>rain let up</strong>.

Rain is usually accompanied by wind, which can be <strong>light</strong>/<strong>slight winds</strong> or <strong>high</strong>/<strong>strong winds</strong>. If the wind is especially strong and violent, you can say <strong>fierce winds</strong> or <strong>gale-force winds</strong> – this last expression is usually used for winds at the level of a hurricane. When the wind is increasing in intensity, we say "<strong>The wind is picking up</strong>," and when it is decreasing in intensity, we say "<strong>The wind is dying down</strong>."

Sometimes we describe the wind with an adjective that emphasizes the cold – such as a <strong>biting wind</strong> or an <strong>icy wind</strong>. Finally, a sudden instance of wind can be called a <strong>blast of wind</strong> or a <strong>gust of wind</strong>.

Snow, like rain, can be <strong>light</strong> or <strong>heavy</strong>. Light snow can also be called <strong>snow flurries</strong>. When the snow is being blown around in the wind, we can describe it as <strong>driving snow</strong> or <strong>swirling snow</strong>. It can be wonderful to walk through <strong>fresh</strong>, <strong>powdery</strong>, <strong>newly-fallen snow</strong> – but not so nice to drive on it. So we <strong>clear the snow</strong> or <strong>plough the snow</strong> with vehicles to remove it from the roads. You can also do this manually – that's called <strong>shoveling the snow</strong>. At the end of winter, when temperatures rise, the snow <strong>melts</strong> – it turns into water and disappears.

Sometimes the weather is unusual for a particular time of year. For example, in the winter, you might have a day that is <strong>unseasonably warm</strong>, and in the summer, you might have a day that is <strong>unseasonably cool</strong>. When the temperature reaches an extreme, we call that a <strong>record high</strong> or a <strong>record low</strong>.
