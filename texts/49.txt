Lesson 49 – Confusing Words

We've already looked at a few pairs of confusing words, like end and finish, big and large, or fast and quick. Here are some additional verbs that are frequently confused by English learners, with their different collocations explained.

**close / shut**

Close and shut are the same for doors, windows, mouths, and eyes.
<strong>Close the door</strong> / <strong>Shut the door</strong> so that the mosquitoes don't get in.
It's cold in here; could you please <strong>close the window</strong> / <strong>shut the window</strong>? She <strong>shut her eyes</strong> / <strong>closed her eyes</strong> as she began to meditate.
Dan's mouth was <strong>closed / shut</strong>.

Note: The word "shut" can be used with a word that implies the action was noisy:
She <strong>slammed the door shut</strong> (closed door with loud noise).
The wind made the gate <strong>bang shut</strong> (closed with loud bang).

Note: The phrase "<strong>Shut your mouth!</strong>" is a rude way to tell somebody to stop talking.

Close (not shut) is used for shops, banks, etc. as well as for airports and roads that are not open or available at a particular time:
What time does the <strong>bank close</strong>?
The roads are <strong>closed due to construction</strong>. The shop is <strong>closing in 30 minutes</strong>.

We use expressions with "close" to refer to the end of an event or process:
Let's <strong>bring this meeting to a close</strong> (end the meeting).
It appears that the economic crisis is <strong>drawing to a close</strong> / <strong>coming to a close</strong> (ending). Messi scored a goal in the <strong>closing moments</strong> (final moments) of the game.

**gain / win / earn**

The word win means to be #1 in a competition, or to receive an award.
My soccer team <strong>won the game</strong> 3-1. I want to <strong>win the lottery</strong>!
John <strong>won a prize</strong> in the science competition.

You can <strong>win a game</strong>, <strong>win a race</strong>, <strong>win a competition</strong>, or <strong>win the lottery</strong>. You can also <strong>win a medal</strong> (like in the Olympics), <strong>a prize</strong>, or <strong>an award</strong>. In conflicts, you can <strong>win an argument</strong>, <strong>win a battle</strong> or <strong>win the war</strong>.

The word earn means to get something in exchange for your work or effort.
Sarah is a famous lawyer; she <strong>earns a lot of money</strong>. I'm not rich, but I <strong>earn a decent salary</strong>.
My bank account <strong>earns 2% interest</strong> per month.
The salesman <strong>earns a 10% commission</strong> on every sale he makes.

The word gain means to receive/obtain or to increase (it may or may not be the result of work or effort).
I've <strong>gained weight</strong> ever since I stopped exercising.
Jack's car <strong>gained speed</strong> as he drove down the mountain.
This training has helped me <strong>gain insight</strong> into modern educational methods. In my last job, I <strong>gained a lot of experience</strong>.
He has <strong>gained a reputation</strong> for complete honesty in his business dealings.

**save / keep**

Save is used for conserving something – you can <strong>save energy</strong>, <strong>save time</strong>, or <strong>save space</strong>. It can also be used for rescuing, as in the expression "<strong>save someone's life</strong>." The expression "<strong>save money</strong>" has two meanings - to conserve money by paying less, for example: "Coupons help me save money at the supermarket." It also means to keep money for a long time, for example: "I'm <strong>saving money</strong> for my vacation in December." In this second case, we often say "<strong>saving up money</strong>."

The word keep means to maintain something in your possession – for example, if someone gives you a jacket, and you try to return it, but the other person tells you to <strong>keep it</strong>. There are various other expressions using keep in the sense of maintaining or continuing:
<strong>keep trying</strong> = continue trying
<strong>keep an eye on</strong> / <strong>keep track of</strong> = continue to observe/monitor a situation
<strong>keep in mind</strong> = continue being aware of something
<strong>keep in touch</strong> = maintain contact with a person
<strong>keep something clean/safe</strong>, <strong>keep someone happy/quiet</strong> = maintain the object or person in that state
<strong>keep a promise</strong> = do the action that you promised to do
<strong>keep a secret</strong> = maintain a secret hidden, not tell it to anybody

**spend / pay**

You <strong>spend money</strong> and <strong>time</strong>:
I <strong>spent $100 on tickets</strong> to the baseball game.
We <strong>spent three hours waiting</strong> in line to buy the tickets!

You can also <strong>spend a period of time</strong> like the morning, afternoon, night, weekend, week, etc.
I <strong>spent the weekend</strong> making repairs to the house.
The students <strong>spent the afternoon</strong> volunteering at the animal shelter.

You pay the request, bill, or demand for the money: <strong>pay rent</strong>, <strong>pay a fine</strong>, <strong>pay your bills</strong>, <strong>pay your taxes</strong>

It's also possible to use pay with quantities of money, but we use a different preposition:
I spent $100 on the tickets. I <strong>paid $100 for the tickets</strong>.
